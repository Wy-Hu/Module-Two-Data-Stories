# Module 2 Data Stories

## Original source
The source for the project is:
**The Largest Vocabulary in Hip Hop** by **The Pudding**:
https://pudding.cool/projects/vocabulary/

The original piece is built around a powerful but relatively direct premise: rank rappers by the number of unique words they use, then let readers explore that hierarchy. My version keeps the same source topic and dataset lineage, but changes the editorial question. Instead of asking who has the largest vocabulary overall, I ask whether raw vocabulary totals are enough to explain lyrical distinction across different eras of hip-hop.

## What I changed from The Pudding
The biggest change is the argument. The Pudding’s project foregrounds total vocabulary size as the main point of comparison. My redesign treats that all-time ranking as only a starting point, then challenges it by introducing era as a necessary frame of interpretation.

I changed the story in several connected ways:

- I shifted the core claim from absolute size to relative distinction.
- I reorganized the experience into a three-part argumentative sequence rather than a broad exploratory presentation.
- I added era-based comparison logic by calculating how far each rapper sits above or below their era’s median vocabulary.
- I replaced a purely all-time interpretation with an era-adjusted ranking.
- I introduced coordinated interaction so one artist can be traced across all views.
- I rewrote the page as a standalone narrative article rather than a project writeup.

Together, these changes move the piece away from “who used the most words?” and toward “who most exceeded the vocabulary norms of their own time?”

## Redesign process
My approach to the redesign was an editorial reframing, not a reskin. There were four stages in the process.

### 1. Identify the hidden assumption in the original story
The first step in the process was to identify what the original story assumes. What the original list assumes is that there is one vocabulary standard that can measure every artist. This assumption is what I chose to attack in the redesign.

The assumption that there is one vocabulary standard that can measure every artist in hip-hop history led me to the central question of the project.

If every era in hip-hop history has vocabulary norms unique to that time, then what does vocabulary measure?

### 2. Turn the critique into a story structure
With the critique in place, I constructed the story in such a way that each section advances the argument one step.

1. Begin with the original list, as it is more intuitive and easier to understand.
2. Introduce the critique by showing each artist relative to the norms of their own era.
3. Show the results of the critique by re-ranking the artists.

The structure of the piece is as much a design decision as it is a writing decision. I wanted the reader to have an expectation, an expectation that the original list is correct, and then slowly come to see why that expectation isn't quite complete.

### 3. Redesign the visuals based on the argument
I chose three types of charts for the piece, each for a different rhetorical function.

- A horizontal bar chart maintains the traditional authority of a list.
- A decade-based distribution perspective implies that "normal" vocabulary changes over time.
- A slope chart makes it easy to visualize the ranks moving over time once context from the era has been added.

These are not just variations in chart type for variety’s sake. Each type has been chosen because it reflects a different part of the argument. The flow of the visualizations reflects the flow of the argument.

### 4. Make the story readable as an article, not just an interaction demo
I have edited the text to make it readable as an article, not just an interaction demo. I have removed the repeated statements of purpose for each chart. I have also changed the layout to stacked, rather than side by side, to improve the flow of reading. In each section of argument, the text comes first, then the visualization.

This aligns with the purpose of the project to make it possible to read like an independent piece of work, not like a work written for the purpose of demonstrating how to do this, or like a work written to showcase charts.

## Design decisions and why they matter

### Reframing the metric
I have kept the vocabulary totals because they are still relevant. They are descriptive. I have not kept them because I think they are decisive. I think this design decision helps to retain the interest generated by the original metric, while allowing room to question it.

### Adding era baselines
I have added each rapper’s position against the median vocabulary for his or her era. I think this has been the most important part of the analysis. It’s the part that gives context visibility.

Without this, the story can only describe the process of accumulation. With this, the story can describe deviation from the norm.

### Changing the final comparison
I did not want the project to end on another version of the same leaderboard. So, the final slope chart directly compares raw rank and era-adjusted rank. This makes the redesign’s claim concrete: once context matters, the hierarchy changes.

### Coordinated interaction
I added hover, click-to-highlight, and era filters that are shared across all views. These interactions are meant to help, not hinder, comparison. A user can follow an artist through the entire process and test whether the argument holds at the all-time and era-specific levels.

### Narrative tone and layout
I took out assignment-style explanation from the page itself and kept the production rationale in this README. I also changed the layout so that the narration is no longer parallel to the visualizations. Each section now reads in a linear fashion, where the prose makes the claim and the visualization is the supporting evidence.

That was an intentional storytelling decision. This page is meant to read like an argument with receipts, not a dashboard.

## Data and transformation
The project uses the uploaded hip-hop vocabulary dataset from the Tahir Hemphill source material. I retained the key fields for artist, era, and vocabulary count, and added the derived fields that are necessary for the redesign:

* `aboveEraMedian`
* `rawRank`
* `relativeRank`

For the final slope chart, I also compute the raw rank and era-adjusted rank within the currently visible selection so that the rank shift annotations match the positions that are currently on screen.

## Final takeaway
The redesign assumes that the raw totals of vocabulary aren’t necessarily meaningless, but they’re incomplete. A large vocabulary might indicate a level of scale, but it doesn’t necessarily account for distinction when artists come from different historical contexts of language use. 

So, the biggest difference between the two versions of the text is the way the text approaches the idea of era. My version doesn’t use it as a way to contextualize the data, but as a way to give the data meaning. According to this logic, the more important question isn’t necessarily which artists used the most unique words, but which artists used the most unique words relative to the expectations of their own era. 

## Addendum 1: How the approach changes the reader experience
The redesign doesn’t just change the argument. It changes the way a reader experiences and tests the argument as well. 

The way the argument is presented in the original Pudding text puts the reader in a position to explore the “best of all time” list and to internalize the vocabulary metric as a compelling way to measure the artists. This works as a compelling way to present the argument because it’s immediate. There’s a sense of stability to the hierarchy and a sense of objectivity to the metric. There’s a sense of flow to the text, so the reader can move through the argument and feel like the question has already been answered. 

This shift has several effects on the engagement:

- It slows down judgment. The reader is less likely to assume that the initial leaderboard is correct without revision, as they are being taught to expect revision.
- It makes comparison more active. The coordinated highlighting and filtering encourage the reader to test the argument artist by artist, and era by era.
- It turns context into evidence. The historical context is no longer something that is used to explain the chart after it is created, but becomes something that is used to challenge the initial ranking.
- It makes the consequences more significant. When the final ranking changes, the reader is reminded that they could have been misled by relying on the initial metric.

In short, the redesigned version changes the role of the reader. They are no longer just reading a ranked list, but are being led through an engagement that involves doubt, comparison, and reinterpretation. The subject of the piece is no longer “what is the winner?” but “how does the metric influence, and mislead, the interpretation of the data?”

## Addendum 2: View coordination, multiple views, and the counterargument
Another significant part of the redesign is the use of multiple coordinated views to build the counterargument, not just through text, but visually.

This is because the original list is complete as is. A ranked list is seen as a way to settle the issue once and for all. My revised version tries to combat this sense of closure by fragmenting the argument across multiple views, each with a distinct argumentative role. The first view displays the original logic at its strongest. The second view enters the contextual evidence that undercuts the logic. The third view reveals the consequence of the counterargument by fundamentally changing the original ranking.

The view coordination also serves to emphasize this argument. The user can select an artist in a view and then see how that artist changes in other views. The artist will remain the same, but the context in which they are viewed will change. This is exactly how the counterargument is presented. The new views in this project are meant to illustrate that it’s not the rapper that’s changing; it’s the standard by which he’s being judged.

This has a number of important implications for how a user will engage with this project:

- It makes comparisons an integral part of the project rather than an afterthought.
- It makes it easier to see how a single artist changes in all views.
- It makes it so that the final argument is not arbitrary because it’s grounded in all of the previous views.
- It makes it so that the counterargument is testable because a user can see for themselves how an artist changes in all views.

Therefore, it’s clear that this project’s use of coordinated views is not merely a cosmetic choice. Rather, it’s a necessary part of how this argument is presented. The project is using coordinated views to take a user from a place of agreement with the initial view to a place of understanding why a new view is more accurate in its assessment of lyrical skill.
