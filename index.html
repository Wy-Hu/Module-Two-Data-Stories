Do the most lyrical rappers really have the biggest vocabulary?

Counting unique words is one way to measure lyrical range. But if each era of hip-hop has its own language norms, then a raw all-time ranking can miss what makes an artist exceptional in context.

By Wenyang Hu

The use of vocabulary as a measure of the complexity of rap lyrics is a well-established practice. There are many reasons for this, but the most important reason might be the fact that using vocabulary as a measure of the complexity of rap lyrics lends itself to a numerical value, and everything numerical lends itself to a decisive conclusion. If rapper A uses more unique words than rapper B, then it would be natural to conclude that rapper A must be more skilled, more ambitious, or more talented than rapper B.

However, the reality of rap music and the reality of the world of rap lyrics is the fact that rap music and rap lyrics have never existed in a world of fixed and stable linguistic conditions. Each era of rap music has its own conditions, its own norms, and its own expectations. Therefore, the fact that a rapper might use a large number of unique words might tell us how much vocabulary the rapper used, but it would never tell us whether that vocabulary use was ordinary for its own era or whether it was unusually large for its own era.

The purpose of the text being written and the purpose of the text being read is to establish the fact that the absolute ranking of rappers by the number of unique words they use might be descriptive but would never be decisive. Therefore, to establish the essence of the complexity of rap lyrics, the data must consider the question of how much a rapper's vocabulary use stretched beyond the norms of its own era.

Step 1

Raw scale is persuasive

The original logic of the all-time vocabulary ranking is persuasive for a reason. It translates lyrical range into a numerical hierarchy, and numerical hierarchies feel conclusive. If one rapper used more unique words than another, it is easy to treat that result as evidence of greater lyrical achievement.

This first view preserves that original claim on purpose. It shows why the argument is compelling, but it also reveals the assumption underneath it: that every artist can be judged against the same world of language, regardless of era.

Step 2

Context changes what counts as exceptional

When each artist is put back in their own world of language, their own decade, then the meaning of the statistics changes. What one artist says, and how that relates to what others said, becomes more relevant. What matters is how far each artist rose above what others said in their own time.

This second view is the counterargument to the original ranking. Once each artist is compared to the baseline of their own era, the all-time ordering stops looking neutral. Some artists are helped by being judged in comparison to decades where the average artist said less, and others are harmed by being judged in comparison to decades where the average artist said more.

Step 3

Relative advantage tells a sharper story

The last comparison asks the toughest and most interesting question of all. Which artists shine after accounting for their historical context? When each artist is ranked based on how far they rose above their own decade’s average, the rankings are not the same.

These changes are the refutation. The movement between the two rankings shows that the original all-time hierarchy cannot be treated as the final word on lyrical greatness. What matters is not only what an artist said, but how far they rose above what others said in their own time.

Conclusion

The strongest conclusion that can be drawn from these statistics is that, while the statistics are not wrong, they are incomplete. They can be used to describe the scale of an artist’s language, but they cannot be used to describe their greatness.

If one considers the entirety of rap as one timeless pool of language, then the individuals with the largest totals will always seem to be the ones who tell the whole story. However, once one considers each era as their own benchmark, then the more precise notion of greatness comes into view. The most compelling way to look at the matter is not based on the individuals who used the most unique words overall. It is based on the individuals who most exceeded the vocabulary norms of their time.
